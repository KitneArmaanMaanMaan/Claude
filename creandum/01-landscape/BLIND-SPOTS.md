# Blind-spot audit — why the first pass produced a consensus answer

## The measurable failure

**153 of 153 companies on the longlist had already been validated by someone else** before I
saw them — 103 through Y Combinator's selection, 50 through an announced funding round
covered by European tech press. There was not one company in the set that no investor had
yet blessed.

That is not a sampling accident. It is what the sourcing channels *are*. A funding
announcement is a record of consensus having already formed. Y Combinator is the largest
consensus-manufacturing institution in startups. I built a funnel that could only return
pre-approved companies, and then picked the best one in it — which is how you arrive at
Squid: excellent, defensible, and agreed-with.

## Six specific blind spots

**1. I searched for what is hot, never for what is hated.**
Every query was of the form "X raises seed 2026". Not one asked what the market has written
off. Contrarian companies do not appear in "top startups to watch" lists — that is the
definition of contrarian.

**2. I never went upstream of the funding event.**
The plan named registries, grant winners, patents, arXiv, Hugging Face, GitHub, and
university spinout pipelines as Tier 1 precisely because deep-tech teams appear there *years*
before a round. I used Companies House to *verify* a finalist and skipped the rest entirely.
Those are the only channels where a company nobody believes in yet is visible.

**3. I anchored on Creandum's revealed preference, which is consensus by construction.**
80% of their 2025–26 deals are AI-tagged and >90% of the Euro Seed 50 is AI-native. Scoring
"Creandum fit" against that record mechanically rewards companies that look like the last
ten deals. Their *stated* posture — "before it's obvious", invest at 80% conviction — points
the opposite way, and I weighted the revealed behaviour over the stated intent.

**4. I had no way to measure how contrarian anything was.**
The rubric scored "why now" and "moat" but nothing asked: *what does the market believe that
this company's existence denies?* Without that dimension, a consensus company with good
metrics always outscores a heretical one with few.

**5. I read summaries, not artifacts.**
Most evidence came through search-result snippets. Someone doing something genuinely strange
is legible in their paper, their repo, their patent or their demo — not in a press summary,
because there usually isn't one.

**6. I treated "unfashionable sector" as a proxy for "non-consensus bet".**
Grid software is an unfashionable *category*. But Squid's technical thesis — that AI needs a
clean governed data layer first — is something every investor nods along to. Unfashionable
market, orthodox belief. The two are not the same thing, and conflating them is the core
error of the first pass.

## What replaces it

A different question drives the second pass. Not "is this a good company in a quiet market"
but:

> **What does this company believe that almost everyone qualified to have an opinion believes
> is wrong? And what would have to be true for the crowd to be the ones who are mistaken?**

Sourcing therefore moves entirely upstream of funding: research preprints, open-source
repositories, patent filings, non-dilutive grant awards, university spinout registers, and
the specific technical areas the market has publicly declared dead.
