What the market assumes

Compute is fabricated in a foundry. The only live argument is which silicon — GPU, TPU, ASIC, photonic, analog, neuromorphic. Roughly $400B a year is being deployed on that assumption.

What Frontier claims

The substrate is wrong. Grow living neuronal tissue and train on that. Memory and compute are colocated inside the cell, so the bottleneck the whole GPU industry engineers around is simply absent.

01 The heresy

The market's verdict on biological computing is not neutral, it is dismissive. When Cortical Labs' DishBrain learned Pong in 2022 it was covered as a curiosity, not a roadmap. Four years on, no serious ML team trains anything on neurons. The settled view is that wetware is a neuroscience instrument that makes good press, and that anyone claiming otherwise has not understood how far behind it is.

That consensus may well be right — §05 argues it as hard as I can. But it is worth examining what the consensus is actually built on.

02 Why the crowd might be wrong

Wetware did not stall because biological computing failed. It stalled on a plumbing problem.

Cultured neural tissue develops a necrotic core once it passes roughly 300–400 µm, because oxygen and nutrients diffuse no further than a few hundred micrometres without a blood supply. Past that geometric limit, the middle of your tissue dies. Every scaling ceiling in the field traces back to that one fact. The field never concluded that the idea was wrong — it hit vascularisation and stopped.

That is a biology problem, and biology has been working on it. Protocols published in 2025–26 now grow cortical organoids past 3 mm without necrotic cores, using endothelial co-culture, microfluidic perfusion and 3D bioprinting. The blocker that produced the consensus is being dismantled in academic labs right now — which means the question the market treats as settled was settled against an obstacle that is going away.

Meanwhile the case for caring has strengthened. The binding constraint on frontier training moved from raw FLOPs to memory bandwidth and energy. A single training GPU draws 700–2,700 W and runs for weeks; datacentre buildout is now gated on grid connections, with ~1,700 GW of projects queued across Europe. A human brain runs ~86 billion neurons on about 20 watts.

Nobody sane claims neurons replace GPUs. The claim worth underwriting is narrower: that a class of work exists — continual learning, adaptation without catastrophic forgetting, learning from very few trials — where a substrate that learns the way brains learn is not a thousand times behind but ahead. If that class exists and is commercially useful, whoever grows the substrate at scale owns a category with no incumbent.

05 The bear case

Argued as hard as I can

  1. The hardware dies. Cortical Labs keeps neurons alive roughly six months on active life support. A compute substrate with a half-life measured in months has an operating model nobody has solved.
  2. The clock gap is nine orders of magnitude. Neurons fire at Hz; transistors switch at GHz. Parallelism is the offered answer, but it does not rescue every workload and the gap is not a detail.
  3. Reproducibility. No two cultures are identical. ML as practised needs deterministic, repeatable training runs. A substrate that answers differently each time is a research instrument, not infrastructure.
  4. The central claim is unpublished. No peer review, no preprint, no independent replication. A Frogger video is not evidence of a solved diffusion limit.
  5. Frogger is not training. DishBrain played Pong in 2022. Four years and one more game later, the distance to "ML training substrate" is not incremental — it is the whole problem. Neuron count is not capability.
  6. Team of one. A nineteen-year-old solo founder, scientific leadership unfilled, attempting a problem that has defeated well-funded academic groups.
  7. Ethics is a live wire. Human-derived neural tissue at 100M+ neurons walks into an unresolved bioethics debate about sentience. That threatens LP comfort, university partnerships and licence to operate — and it gets worse precisely as the technology succeeds.
  8. 75%+ with board control is evidence of conviction and also a governance risk. There is no mechanism by which investors correct a wrong turn.

The single fact that would most change the conclusion: whether the vascularisation result survives contact with an independent lab. Everything else is downstream of it.

08 What I am not claiming

I am not claiming this is the highest-expected-value company found. Squid — grid-network model-of-record, in production at National Grid, also unpriced — is far more likely to return capital. That memo stands as the low-variance alternative.

The narrower claim is this: Squid's central belief is one the market already shares, so it cannot produce a non-consensus outcome. If the brief is to find the company doing what almost everyone qualified believes is wrong, Frontier Computing is that company and Squid is not. Those are different questions with different right answers, and the fund should know which one it is asking.

How this pick was reached

The first pass longlisted 153 European companies and 153 of 153 had already been validated by Y Combinator or by an announced round covered in the tech press. That funnel can only return companies someone has already agreed with, which is how it produced a competent, orthodox answer.

The second pass scored candidates on a different axis — how wrong the market thinks they are — and sourced upstream of any funding event: company registries, arXiv, the biology literature, and the specific technical areas the market has publicly written off. Neuromorphic computing and formal verification were screened out as no longer contrarian; AMI Labs and Biomemory on stage and ownership.

Every factual claim is traced to a URL with an access date in the accompanying source appendix. Six items are explicitly flagged as unverified, including the one the entire thesis rests on.
